I leave home and walk to work.

I know the route well enough that my legs can apparently complete it without
much involvement from the rest of me. There are roads to cross, signs to follow
and buildings on either side, but most mornings they pass like scenery left
over from the day before.

The journey is the same. The walk is not.

## The first walk

I leave home and walk to work, repeating Islamic duas under my breath.

The words give the walk a quiet rhythm. I am moving through the city, but my
attention is not entirely on it. Road names and buildings still register enough
for me to get where I am going, while the repetition draws part of me inward.

This is not an empty walk. It is occupied, but gently. There is space between
the words. My surroundings remain present without demanding to be the main
event.

By the time I reach work, the route has done two jobs. It has carried me to a
destination and given me a little private space before the day begins.

## The second walk

I leave home and walk to work with nothing playing.

At first, this can feel like forgetting something. My phone is there. My
headphones are available. There is an entire supply of music waiting to rescue
me from the terrible prospect of simply walking down a road.

Without it, the route begins to offer its own material.

I read the names on street signs rather than using them only as navigation. I
look at the buildings and notice their architecture: how one differs from the
next, what belongs together and what looks as though it arrived from another
idea of the city. Things that were previously just “the way to work” become
individual things again.

Nothing dramatic has happened. I have not discovered a hidden courtyard or
been handed the secret meaning of life by a stranger at a crossing. I have just
looked at a route I take regularly.

That is enough to make it feel less like dead time.

## The third walk

I leave home and walk to work with music playing.

This is the version I know best. Music changes the pace and mood of the journey.
It can make an ordinary commute more enjoyable. It can also absorb me so
completely that I arrive remembering the music and almost none of the walk.

The road signs were there. So were the buildings. I must have passed them,
because I have successfully appeared at work rather than wandered into another
part of Edinburgh. But the journey itself has left very little trace.

This happens to me all the time. I do not decide that I want to ignore the
route. I simply put music on, and attention follows it. My body handles the
commute while my mind goes somewhere else.

## Choosing the walk

I used to think about walks mainly in terms of destinations. A useful walk got
me somewhere; the time in between was available to be filled. Music made the
journey better. News or an audiobook could make it productive. Silence was the
option left over when I had not chosen anything else.

I am starting to think that the unfilled walk is its own choice.

That does not mean I should stop listening to music, or that every commute must
become an exercise in intense observation. Some mornings I will want a song.
On others, repeating duas may be the purpose of the walk. Sometimes I may want
to notice the road names and architecture that have been patiently existing
beside me the whole time.

The difference is small: deciding what kind of attention I want to give before
I put my headphones on.

For now, my experiment is simply to leave some walks unfilled. Not every walk,
and not because empty time is morally superior. I just want to find out how
much of my ordinary life I have been moving through without really seeing it.
